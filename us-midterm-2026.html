2026 U.S. Midterms: Democrats Win Congress — Balance of Power and Race Calls

U.S. Midterm Elections · Election Night and Morning-After Coverage · Updated: Nov. 5, 2026, 11:29 PM JST (9:29 AM EST, Nov. 5)

Democrats have won control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate in the 2026 midterm elections, which major U.S. outlets describe as a rebuke of President Donald Trump. This page synthesizes race calls, partial returns, and voter poll findings from NBC News, CBS News, CNBC, and CNN. Counting continues in Alaska, Mississippi, and dozens of House races.

Decision Desk Notice (11:29 PM JST, Nov. 5 / 9:29 AM EST, Nov. 5): Both chambers have been called for Democrats. In the House, NBC News has called 249 Democratic and 158 Republican seats, with 28 races uncalled, and still estimates Democrats will finish with about 259 seats (218 needed); CNBC's Election Desk, in an earlier check, said at least 250 Democratic and 160 Republican. In the Senate, NBC's tally shows Democrats at 54 seats (counting the two independents who caucus with them) and Republicans at 44, with only Alaska and Mississippi uncalled (the Senate seat count has not changed since the earlier update, and only the Alaska and Mississippi vote shares moved; the House tally moved from 242 Democratic, 151 Republican, and 42 uncalled at 12:46 AM EST to 249, 158, and 28). Democrats flipped seven Republican-held seats (North Carolina, South Carolina, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas, Texas, and Maine) and held Michigan, Georgia, Minnesota, New Hampshire, and Virginia. Republicans held Florida and Nebraska. All figures are preliminary and will change as counting continues. CNN's own pages could not be retrieved directly; CNN's position below comes from its published headline and summaries.

1Key Takeaways

As of 9:29 AM EST on Nov. 5 (11:29 PM JST, Nov. 5), the night's result is clear: Democrats have won control of both chambers of Congress for the final two years of President Donald Trump's term, in an election that outlets from CBS to CNN frame as a rebuke of the president.[7][5]

In the House, CBS News projected the Democratic win at 10:15 PM EST, and NBC News and CNN called it within minutes. NBC News's running count stands at 249 Democratic seats called (a net gain of 40) against 158 Republican, with 28 races still uncalled; it still estimates Democrats will finish with about 259 seats and Republicans about 176 (218 needed). CNBC's Election Desk, at an earlier check, projected at least 250 Democratic and 160 Republican seats.[6][1][7]

In the Senate, CBS News projected Democratic control shortly after 11:30 PM EST, and NBC News and CNBC's Election Desk now show the same. NBC's tally has Democrats at 54 seats (counting the two independents who caucus with them) and Republicans at 44; CNBC's Election Desk now projects at least 54 Democrats and at least 42 Republicans, well past the 51 needed for control even though Vice President JD Vance would break a 50-50 tie. Democrats began the night needing a net gain of four seats. They flipped seven: North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, Ohio, Kansas, Iowa, and Maine. They also held Michigan, Georgia, Minnesota, New Hampshire, and Virginia. Republicans held Florida (Ashley Moody) and Nebraska (Pete Ricketts, over independent Dan Osborn). Only Alaska and Mississippi remain uncalled.[2][7][1]

Voter polls point the same way. CBS News exit polling put Trump's approval at 38%, on track for the lowest midterm approval for a president in CBS exit polling, and about three-quarters of voters were at least dissatisfied with the country's direction. In the CNBC Voter Poll, 59% said their House vote was meant to express support or opposition to Trump (43% against, 16% for), 62% said inflation is due more to Trump's policies, and 65% said tariffs hurt the national economy. The NBC News exit poll found 62% of voters disapproving of Trump.[7][1][6]

2How the Midterms Work

Held at the midpoint of a president's four-year term, midterm elections determine the composition of the United States Congress. While the presidency is not on the ballot, shifts in congressional control reshape legislative priorities, federal spending, subpoena and oversight powers, and the confirmation of judicial and executive nominees.

Before the vote, Reuters framed the question as whether either party would win unified control or Washington would fracture into divided government in 2027. The answer is divided government: Republicans keep the White House, and Democrats hold both chambers.[3]

4Voter Priorities

The CNBC Voter Poll, fielded across Election Day, highlights frustration centered on the economy and the president. About 75% rated the economy “not so good” or “poor,” and 79% said the cost of living causes them severe or moderate hardship.[1]

Voters were also asked about foreign policy: 74% said Iran was a crucial or major factor in their congressional vote, and 62% said military action there was the wrong decision. On candidates, 61% of Texas voters viewed Ken Paxton negatively, 58% of Maine voters said Susan Collins is too aligned with Trump (while 56% said Troy Jackson lacks the temperament to serve), and 58% of North Carolina voters said Michael Whatley was too close to Trump.[1]

The NBC News exit poll points the same way: 62% of voters disapprove of Trump, more than three-quarters disapprove of Congress, and nearly half see the election as a chance to rebuke the president. Latino voters backed Democrats for the House 60% to 35% (Kamala Harris won 51% in 2024), and the economy was the top issue for Latino voters (45%) and Black voters (50%). State-level findings include 65% of Texas voters saying Ken Paxton lacks the right personal character, 63% of South Carolina voters saying Graham lacks the right experience, and 55% of Kansas voters saying Sen. Roger Marshall lacks the right personal character.[6]

CBS News exit polling adds that Trump's approval was underwater in nearly all Senate battleground states, and that 69% of Maine voters said Trump's tariffs had harmed their local economy.[7]

5Key Senate Races

Democrats needed a net gain of four seats and ended the night with seven flips, plus holds in Michigan, Georgia, Minnesota, New Hampshire, and Virginia. Percentages below are NBC News tallies from its Senate results page (7:30 AM EST; only Alaska and Mississippi were rechecked at 9:29 AM EST, Nov. 5) and will change as counting finishes.

5.1 Roy Cooper defeats Michael Whatley

North Carolina · Flip
Former Democratic Governor Roy Cooper was the first Senate flip of the night, called at 8:30 PM EST; NBC's tally shows 58.7% to 39.1% (99.0% in). He flipped the seat of retiring Republican Senator Thom Tillis and will be the first Democrat elected to the Senate from North Carolina since Kay Hagan in 2008.[2][4][5][7]

5.2 Jon Ossoff holds his seat

Georgia · Hold
Democrat Jon Ossoff won re-election over Republican Mike Collins, who conceded; CBS News called the race at 7:39 PM EST. NBC's tally shows 59.0% to 41.0% (98.7% in).[1][2][7]

5.3 Chris Pappas defeats John Sununu

New Hampshire · Hold
Democrat Chris Pappas won the open seat, keeping it in Democratic hands (called 8:21 PM EST). NBC's tally shows 56.2% to 41.9% (90.1% in).[2][7]

5.4 James Talarico defeats Ken Paxton

Texas · Flip
Democrat James Talarico defeated Republican state Attorney General Ken Paxton for the seat of Senator John Cornyn. CBS News called the race at 10:55 PM EST, the first Democratic Senate win in Texas since 1988, and Cornyn congratulated Talarico. NBC's tally shows 54.9% to 43.2% (93.7% in). The Texas governor's race also flipped, with Gina Hinojosa defeating incumbent Greg Abbott.[2][7][1]

5.5 Abdul El-Sayed defeats Mike Rogers

Michigan · Hold
For retiring Senator Gary Peters' seat, Democrat Abdul El-Sayed defeated former Republican Representative Mike Rogers, keeping the seat in Democratic hands; CBS News called it at 2:30 AM EST. NBC's tally shows 52.3% to 45.7% (94.4% in).[2][7]

5.6 Sherrod Brown defeats Jon Husted

Ohio · Flip
In the special election, Democrat Sherrod Brown defeated Republican Jon Husted, called at 11:11 PM EST. NBC's tally shows 49.7% to 45.5% (97.3% in). Democrat Amy Acton also won the governor's race over Vivek Ramaswamy.[2][7][1]

5.7 Annie Andrews defeats Darline Graham

South Carolina · Flip
NBC News (9:58 PM EST) and CNN projected Democrat Annie Andrews as the winner over Republican Sen. Darline Graham, an upset and the first Democratic Senate seat in South Carolina since 1998. NBC's tally shows 51.1% to 46.7% (99.0% in).[1][2][5]

5.8 Troy Jackson defeats Susan Collins

Maine · Flip
The race that looked even at 10:19 PM EST broke for Democrat Troy Jackson. Republican Susan Collins conceded, and CBS News called the race at 12:52 AM EST. NBC's tally shows 53.8% to 46.1% (85.7% in). In the exit poll, 69% of Maine voters said Trump's tariffs had harmed the local economy.[2][7]

6The Stakes and Policy Implications

With both chambers called for Democrats, subpoena and oversight powers over the Trump administration will pass to incoming Democratic committee chairs when the 120th Congress convenes, and a Democratic Senate controls the confirmation calendar for federal judges and executive appointees. The Senate margin is wider than the 51 seats needed, but it is still short of the 60 votes required to overcome a filibuster, and the president retains his veto.

The reaction so far shows how each side reads the result. Trump said he would accept the results and told CBS he would “work with” Democrats, while also posting that he “wasn't on the Ballot” and that if he were, “we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!” Senate Republican leader John Thune said Senate Republicans would push back against Democratic priorities. Chuck Schumer said Democrats won the Senate “against all odds,” Hakeem Jeffries said “America is back,” and Nancy Pelosi endorsed Jeffries for speaker. Iran's parliament speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, linked the results to Americans' rejection of war.[7]

For international allies, including Japan, the critical focus remains congressional influence over tariffs, Pacific defense pacts, and appropriations. Tariffs were a visible liability for the president, with 65% of voters in the CNBC poll saying they hurt the national economy, but Congress's levers are mainly spending and oversight, and the White House keeps its veto. Definitive policy directions will crystallize once the remaining House races are called and the new Congress is seated in January.[1]

7Coffee Break: Why Doesn't an American Party Have a “Leader”?

A side note from the election-night desk. Not part of the race calls.

7.1 Who spoke for the parties on election night?

Scroll back through the night's coverage and a pattern appears. Donald Trump wrote that he “wasn't on the Ballot,” and a senior Republican strategist blamed him for the result. On the Democratic side, Hakeem Jeffries, Chuck Schumer, Nancy Pelosi, and Barack Obama each weighed in on the result, yet none of them is “the leader of the Democratic Party.” A reader used to Japanese or British politics keeps looking for one: the party president (the LDP's sōsai) or party leader who becomes prime minister when the party wins.[7]

The short answer is that American parties do have officials, but no single office that combines what a parliamentary leader holds at once: head of the party organization, chooser of candidates, head of the legislative caucus, and head of government. The closest candidates are these.

7.2 Four structural reasons

  1. Separation of powers. A parliamentary leader's power rests on making or breaking the government. In the United States, the president is elected separately from Congress, cannot be brought down by a vote of no confidence (removal requires impeachment), and cannot dissolve it. The Constitution does not mention parties at all. A member of Congress owes the seat to local voters, so no one in Washington can enforce a party line the way a prime minister can.
  2. Federalism. Each of the 50 states runs its own elections and has its own state party under state law. The national committees were created as coordinating bodies: the DNC in 1848 and the RNC in 1856. The DNC's charter gives it “general responsibility for the affairs of the Democratic Party between National Conventions,” but it sets the rules for caucuses and primaries that the states usually run themselves.[8][10]
  3. Primaries took nominations away from party bosses. After the chaotic 1968 Democratic convention, the DNC's McGovern–Fraser Commission required state parties to open delegate selection, and the result was a shift from insider-run conventions and caucuses to primary elections. Because state primary laws generally cover all parties, Republicans were swept along. George McGovern, who led the commission, later summarized the change: “I opened up the doors of the Democratic Party, and 20 million people walked out.” Voters in each state, not a leader, decide who carries the party's label.[9]
  4. Big tents in a two-party system. This one is my interpretation rather than a finding from the sources. Winner-take-all elections push every faction into one of two tents. A party that has to win Maine, Georgia, and Texas on the same night has to tolerate candidates who differ a great deal, and a leader who enforced one line would risk losing some of those states.

7.3 So who leads when nobody does?

In practice, leadership is occupied rather than appointed. While a party holds the White House, the president is its de facto leader, which is why a bad night for Republicans reads as a verdict on Trump, and why his “wasn't on the Ballot” post is, in effect, an argument about whose verdict this was.[7]

The party out of the White House has no such figure. Its voice is a shifting cast of congressional leaders, governors, former presidents, and, eventually, whoever wins the presidential primaries; the nominee then becomes the party's de facto leader. Pelosi's endorsement of Jeffries for speaker is the more typical way authority is conferred: by the members of one chamber, one office at a time.[7]

So the missing party leader is less a gap than a design choice. The system hands out pieces of leadership to different people and lets a presidency and a primary fill in the rest. For anyone following Washington from Tokyo, the better question is not “Who leads the party?” but “Who controls this particular lever?”
