1. Key Takeaways

As of 9:54 PM EST on Election Night (11:54 AM JST on Nov. 4), voting has concluded in most states, and the returns are shaping the fight for congressional control during the final two years of President Donald Trump's term.

In the House, CNBC's Election Desk says control “leans Democratic.” Democrats are projected to have won at least 210 seats and Republicans at least 160, with 218 needed for a majority. Democrats flipped two Florida districts redrawn to favor Republicans (FL-14 and FL-22) and a North Carolina seat (NC-11).[1]

In the Senate, the AP projected Democrat Roy Cooper as the winner in North Carolina over Republican Michael Whatley, flipping the seat of retiring Republican Thom Tillis. Democrat Jon Ossoff won re-election in Georgia, and Democrat Chris Pappas won the open New Hampshire seat. Republican Ashley Moody was projected to hold the Florida special-election seat. Democrats started the night needing a net gain of four seats for a Senate majority; one flip is confirmed so far.[1][2][4]

The CNBC Voter Poll depicts an electorate focused on the president and the economy: 59% said their House vote was meant to express support or opposition to Trump (43% against, 16% for), 62% said inflation is due more to Trump's policies, and 65% said tariffs hurt the national economy.[1]

2. How the Midterms Work

Held at the midpoint of a president's four-year term, midterm elections determine the composition of the United States Congress. While the presidency is not on the ballot, shifts in congressional control reshape legislative priorities, federal spending, subpoena and oversight powers, and the confirmation of judicial and executive nominees.

The House picture is shaped by mid-decade redistricting. Democrats won Florida's 14th and 22nd districts (Kathy Castor and Pia Dandiya), both redrawn to favor Republicans, and Jared Moskowitz was re-elected in FL-25. In North Carolina's 11th, Democrat Jamie Ager was projected to flip the seat. Republicans Anna Paulina Luna (FL-13) and Maria Elvira Salazar (FL-27) led by under 1 point with over 90% of the vote reporting.[1]

Reuters notes that legislative power in 2027 will depend on whether either party achieves unified control or Capitol Hill fractures into divided government. Even a single-seat shift in either direction would reshape approaching deadlines on statutory spending and the federal debt ceiling.[3]

4. Voter Priorities

The CNBC Voter Poll, fielded across Election Day, highlights frustration centered on the economy and the president. About 75% rated the economy “not so good” or “poor,” and 79% said the cost of living causes them severe or moderate hardship.[1]

Voters were also asked about foreign policy: 74% said Iran was a crucial or major factor in their congressional vote, and 62% said military action there was the wrong decision. On candidates, 61% of Texas voters viewed Ken Paxton negatively, 58% of Maine voters said Susan Collins is too aligned with Trump (while 56% said Troy Jackson lacks the temperament to serve), and 58% of North Carolina voters said Michael Whatley was too close to Trump.[1]

5. Key Senate Races to Watch

Several Senate contests will decide the chamber's majority. Three Democratic wins and one Republican hold have been called; four more races are rated as leaning Democratic, and four are too early to call. Percentages below are NBC News tallies at 9:54 PM EST and will change.

5.1 Roy Cooper defeats Michael Whatley

North Carolina · Flip The AP projected former Democratic Governor Roy Cooper as the winner with 58.8% to 39.1% (90.4% in). He flipped the seat of retiring Republican Senator Thom Tillis, the first Democratic Senate win in North Carolina since 2008.[2][4]

5.2 Jon Ossoff holds his seat

Georgia · Called Democrat Jon Ossoff won re-election over Republican Mike Collins, who conceded. NBC's tally shows 59.0% to 41.0% (79.9% in).[1][2]

5.3 Chris Pappas defeats John Sununu

New Hampshire · Called Democrat Chris Pappas won the open seat, keeping it in Democratic hands. NBC's tally shows 57.6% to 40.6% (46.7% in).[1][2]

5.4 James Talarico vs. Ken Paxton

Texas · Leans Democratic Democrat James Talarico leads Republican state Attorney General Ken Paxton 59.0% to 39.4% (54.9% in) for the seat of Senator John Cornyn. CNBC's Election Desk rates the race as leaning Democratic; it has not been called.[1][2]

5.5 Abdul El-Sayed vs. Mike Rogers

Michigan · Too Early For retiring Senator Gary Peters' seat, Democrat Abdul El-Sayed leads former Republican Representative Mike Rogers 54.3% to 44.0%, but only 18.4% of the vote is in and no call has been made.[2]

5.6 Sherrod Brown vs. Jon Husted

Ohio · Leans Democratic In the special election, Democrat Sherrod Brown leads Republican Jon Husted 48.5% to 46.8% (69.7% in). The race is rated as leaning Democratic but remains uncalled.[1][2]

5.7 Annie Andrews vs. Darline Graham

South Carolina · Leans Democratic Democrat Annie Andrews leads 51.0% to 46.8% (87.5% in). The race is rated as leaning Democratic; a CNBC entry had earlier described it as too close to call.[1][2]

5.8 Susan Collins vs. Troy Jackson

Maine · Too Early Democrat Troy Jackson leads Republican Susan Collins 51.8% to 48.2%, but only 13.8% of the vote is in. Iowa, Nebraska (Dan Osborn, independent, vs. Pete Ricketts), and Alaska (Dan Sullivan vs. Mary Peltola) also remain uncalled.[2]

6. The Stakes and Policy Implications

The Democratic flip in North Carolina and the number of Senate races leaning Democratic underscore the razor-thin arithmetic governing the 120th Congress. If Democrats succeed in flipping either chamber, subpoena and oversight powers will immediately transfer to incoming Democratic committee chairs, creating a formidable legislative counterweight to the Trump administration's second-term policies.

Conversely, if Republicans hold the House and stem further Senate losses, the White House will maintain confirmation pathways for federal judges and executive appointments. For international allies, including Japan, the critical focus remains congressional influence over unilateral trade tariffs, Pacific defense pacts, and statutory appropriations. Definitive policy directions will crystallize once network decision desks project remaining western races.
